# Employee Retention & Performance — Business Insight Report
*Prepared for: Leadership / People Ops · Based on: Employee Retention & Performance Dashboard, 11,991 employees*

---

## The Headline

**1 in 6 employees left the company this period (16.6%) — and the reasons are not random.** People are leaving because they're underpaid, overloaded, or unhappy in their role — three problems the company can act on directly, starting now.

---

## What We Found

- **Pay is the single biggest driver of turnover.** Employees on the lowest pay tier leave 4x more often than employees on the highest pay tier (1 in 5 vs. roughly 1 in 20). If we want to stop losing people, compensation is where to start.

- **Overloaded employees are quitting at an alarming rate.** Employees carrying a heavy workload leave more than half the time (53%) — over five times the rate of employees with a manageable workload (10%). Workload, not pay, is actually the single strongest warning sign we have that someone is about to leave.

- **There's a specific group of people at the highest risk right now.** Roughly 720 employees are both overloaded *and* unhappy in their role at the same time. This group should be treated as urgent — they are the most likely people to hand in notice next.

- **The 5-year mark is a danger zone.** Turnover rises steadily until it peaks at the 5-year mark, where nearly half of employees (45%) leave. After year 6, turnover disappears almost entirely — employees who make it past that point tend to stay long-term. This tells us there's a specific window where people decide whether to commit or move on.

- **Some teams are struggling more than others.** HR has the highest turnover of any department (nearly 1 in 5 employees), while Management and R&D are the most stable (roughly 1 in 8). This suggests the problem isn't company-wide — it's concentrated in specific teams that likely need closer attention.

---

## What We Recommend

1. **Act on the ~720 "overloaded and unhappy" employees first.** This is the clearest, most urgent fire to put out — redistribute their workload or add support before we lose them.
2. **Review pay for lower-tier employees, especially those approaching year 4–5 of tenure.** This is the group most likely to leave over money, right as they hit the highest-risk period for tenure.
3. **Build a "Year 5 check-in."** Since turnover peaks and then nearly vanishes right after, a proactive conversation (comp review, promotion path, workload check) around year 4–5 could be one of the highest-leverage retention moves available.
4. **Give HR, Technical, and Support extra attention.** These teams are losing people faster than the rest of the company and would benefit from a closer look at workload and pay within those specific groups.

---

## How This Analysis Was Done

Before any patterns could be found, the raw employee data had to be cleaned up and organized. Here's what that involved, in plain terms:

- **Checked the data for gaps.** No records were missing, so nothing had to be filled in or estimated.
- **Removed duplicate records.** Just over 3,000 entries turned out to be exact repeats of other employees already in the data — these were removed so no one got counted twice and skewed the numbers.
- **Cleaned up inconsistent labels.** Some entries used different spellings or shorthand for the same thing (for example, one department appeared as "RandD" instead of "Research & Development") — these were standardized so the data could be grouped correctly.
- **Tagged each employee's status.** A simple "stayed" or "left" label was added to every record to make the analysis easier to read and report on.
- **Grouped employees into easy-to-read categories.** Rather than working with raw numbers, employees were sorted into everyday groups — satisfaction (low / medium / high), workload (low / medium / high), and hours worked (low / medium / high) — based on their actual scores and activity. This is what made it possible to spot patterns like "overloaded employees leave 5x more often" at a glance, instead of getting lost in hundreds of individual numbers.

This groundwork is what the findings above are built on — once the data was clean and organized, the patterns around pay, workload, satisfaction, and tenure became clear.

---

## Good to Know Before Acting

- These numbers show *patterns*, not guaranteed causes — for example, we know workload and pay are strongly linked to who leaves, but we don't have exit-interview data confirming *why* each person left.
- This is a snapshot of where things stand today, not a trend over time — a follow-up check next quarter would confirm whether these patterns are worsening, improving, or stable.
